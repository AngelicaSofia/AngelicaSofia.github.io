The [Milano–Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Winter_Olympics) represent far more than a global sporting event. They are a collective endeavor that brings together cities, alpine landscapes, institutions, athletes, and thousands of people working far from the spotlight. For Italy, these Games mark a return to the Winter Olympics after two decades, but also a chance to express a specific vision of hosting: distributed venues, sustainability, and cooperation between very different territories. Milano and Cortina embody this duality—one a modern, international city shaped by innovation and infrastructure, the other a historic mountain town whose identity is deeply tied to winter sports and the Dolomites.

Italy has a long and meaningful relationship with the Winter Olympic Games. The country first hosted them in [Cortina d’Ampezzo 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1956_Winter_Olympics), an edition that helped define the modern image of the Winter Olympics by combining alpine tradition, international visibility, and a strong sense of place. Fifty years later, [Turin 2006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2006_Winter_Olympics) marked a different kind of milestone: a technologically advanced, urban-centered Games that transformed the city’s global identity and demonstrated Italy’s ability to organize complex, large-scale international events. Milano–Cortina 2026 stands at the intersection of these two legacies. It brings together the elegance and mountain heritage of Cortina with the contemporary, global character of Milan, proposing a distributed and sustainable model that reflects how the Winter Games—and Italy itself—have evolved over time.

Winter Olympics have a distinct character compared to their summer counterpart. They are logistically complex, exposed to environmental conditions, and inherently risk-prone. Cold, ice, altitude, speed, and physical impact make safety not just a requirement but a central pillar of the event. Milano–Cortina 2026 will unfold across multiple locations in Northern Italy, requiring precise coordination, mobility, and readiness at all times. Behind every competition lies a system designed to manage uncertainty, respond to emergencies, and protect both athletes and spectators.

## Ice Hockey: Intensity, Discipline, Power & Speed

Among the disciplines featured in the [Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_Olympic_Games), [ice hockey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_hockey_at_the_Olympic_Games) stands out for its intensity. It is fast, physical, and relentless, leaving no margin for distraction. Every shift on the ice depends on flawless timing, teamwork, and discipline—not only from players, but from referees, medical teams, and organizers. Hockey is a sport where collisions are expected, injuries are possible, and rapid intervention can make a crucial difference. Observing it closely makes clear how essential the supporting human infrastructure is to allow the game to unfold safely.

This was particularly true in the context of women’s ice hockey, which represents both **elite competition** and a significant chapter in the **sport’s evolution**. Historically, women were admitted to Olympic ice hockey only at a later stage, with the first official tournament introduced in 1998 at the [Nagano Winter Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1998_Winter_Olympics). Since then, the discipline has grown rapidly in visibility and technical sophistication. Nations such as **Canada** and the **United States** have long dominated the scene, shaping the identity of the sport through intense rivalries, deep development programs, and a culture where women’s hockey is embedded from an early age. In recent years, European teams, particularly from **Scandinavia**, have also gained prominence, contributing to a more competitive and internationally balanced landscape.

What stands out in women’s hockey today is the combination of speed, tactical intelligence, and resilience. The game emphasizes fluidity, anticipation, and collective movement, often highlighting strategic depth as much as physical strength. Seeing these teams compete at Olympic level reinforces how far the sport has come, and how the Games function not only as a stage for excellence, but as a moment of recognition for disciplines that have earned their place through persistence, performance, and cultural change.

## Red Cross: the Invisible Behind the Scenes

In this context, volunteering becomes extremely tangible and concrete. Large sporting events do not function on performance alone; they rely on continuous care, preparedness, and responsibility. Volunteering with the [Red Cross](https://www.icrc.org/en) means operating within that invisible framework that sustains the event from the inside. For volunteers the role is not just symbolic: it is operational, physical, and constant. It means being on duty for service duration uninterrupted hours, ready to intervene, assist, transport, and support—often without pause—while remaining alert, composed, and precise.

Working in transport and medical assistance requires coordination with doctors, nurses, emergency services, and event staff. It means navigating restricted areas, responding to incidents both minor and critical, and adapting to rapidly changing situations. Much of this work happens away from the public eye: corridors, service roads, medical rooms, and backstage zones that spectators never see. Yet it is precisely there that the Olympics are truly lived from within. You experience the rhythm of the event not through applause, but through responsibility.

The Red Cross, and in particular [Italian Red Cross](https://cri.it) in this context, brings into this environment a set of principles that align naturally with the Olympic spirit: humanity, neutrality, service, and preparedness. In practice, these principles translate into presence and reliability. Being available when someone falls, when an athlete is injured, when a spectator feels unwell, or when fatigue sets in. It is a form of participation that is quiet but essential, demanding both technical competence and emotional balance.

What strikes me most about Milano–Cortina 2026 is how clearly it exposes the relationship between excellence and care. Elite sport is often associated with strength, endurance, and achievement, yet it depends profoundly on protection, prevention, and collective responsibility. The success of the Games is not measured only by medals or records, but by how safely and humanely the entire experience unfolds.

Volunteering in this context means contributing to something larger than oneself without needing to be seen. It means living the Olympics from behind the scenes, feeling their pressure, rhythm, and scale, while remaining focused on others. As 2026 approaches, what stays with me is not only the anticipation of competition, but the awareness that the most meaningful experiences are often built quietly—through long hours, steady presence, and the deliberate choice to care.
